# The story continues (2026-08-07 to 2026-08-09)

This document picks up exactly where [`change_story.md`](change_story.md)
left off. That document covered three days (2026-08-04 to 2026-08-07) of
work and ended on an honest, unresolved note: even after finding and fixing
a genuine timing bug, rebuilding the scoring system, and discovering a real
hidden cornering-force ceiling in the real simulator, roughly three-quarters
of the gap between what the offline practice simulator predicted and how the
real car actually drove was still unexplained. This document is the next two
days of chasing that remaining three-quarters — and it does not end with the
gap closed either. It ends with several more real bugs found and fixed, one
of the practice simulator's own long-standing bugs (not the real car's)
found to be a much bigger deal than anyone realised, and a growing list of
findings that point toward the same conclusion from a new direction: the
planner's *plan* is jerkier than the car it plans for.

The same rules apply here as in the first document: each section opens
with bullet points naming the specific problem, fix, and result, followed
by a short paragraph of reasoning for context. Dead ends are reported as
dead ends, and the couple of moments where an earlier finding turned out to
be wrong once looked at more carefully are stated plainly rather than
quietly corrected without comment.

## Part 8 — Someone finally asked the right question: does the practice track match the real one?

- Nobody had ever directly checked whether the offline practice simulator's own test harness calls the shared path-building logic with the *same tuning numbers* the real car uses — only whether the underlying logic files themselves were byte-identical (they were, and still are).
- Checked, and they didn't match: the real car passes four specific numbers into the path-building step (how much to smooth the line, how far ahead to look, and two more); the offline harness's equivalent call passed none of them, silently falling back to the function's own generic defaults. Two of the four happened to match the real car's tuned value purely by coincidence; the other two didn't — most importantly, the offline harness was smoothing the centreline **over three times more heavily** than the real car's own tuned setting.
- **Fixed** by making the offline harness explicitly pass through the same four values the real car uses.
- **Effect: large, and not reassuring.** With the corrected, harder, more realistic line, the offline steering-lock-up rate on the recorded test track more than doubled (under 5% to over 10%), and a test run that used to complete a full lap cleanly now failed partway through. The size of the "unexplained gap" between offline and real driving narrowed from about seventeen-and-a-half percentage points to about eleven — the single biggest reduction of the whole investigation so far, bigger than every previous fix combined.

The underlying planning logic itself had been checked for parity many times before this. What had never been checked was whether the offline test harness *called* that logic with the same settings — identical ingredients cooked with a different recipe can still come out differently, and that's exactly what had been happening this whole time.

## Part 9 — A second, ongoing investigation had been chasing the exact same trail

- A completely separate, parallel investigation had been running on the real car's own code, using real recorded logs instead of the offline simulator — working independently, with no coordination with this document, at the same time.
- Comparing notes: it had found and fixed several of the *same* problems (the missing steering-speed limit, the position-update-timing split, the steering-units bug, the scoring-scale bug, the tiered scoring rebuild, the duplicate-cone bug), and had independently arrived at the same headline number (at least three-quarters of the gap unexplained) and the same next suspect (something wrong in exactly how the path-planner shapes the route).
- Neither side had yet found the too-much-smoothing bug from Part 8 — flagged back to the other investigation rather than left only in this document.
- Paused this document's own re-measurement briefly because the two investigations were, by coincidence, both mid-edit on the exact same braking-distance calculation at the same time. Resumed once that fix was confirmed complete and identical in both places.

This section is mostly bookkeeping, but the coincidence itself is worth recording: two independent investigations converging on the same suspect from different data (offline simulation vs. real logs) is a stronger signal that the suspect is real than either investigation alone would be.

## Part 10 — Chasing one bug into another, into a third: the corner that couldn't be driven

- Re-measured the recorded lap after Part 8's fix plus the concurrent braking-distance fix from Part 9. **It still failed at almost exactly the same point as before** — the braking-distance fix hadn't moved this specific failure at all.
- First theory: the safety feature that pulls target speed down when the visible road ahead gets short (from Part 4/5b) was cutting in too aggressively right at this corner. **Tested directly by temporarily removing it — ruled out immediately**: the failure got worse (one step sooner) and a different, previously-fine test road started failing too. Put the safety feature straight back; it earns its place.
- Found the real cause in the raw path data: the centreline **jumped discontinuously** — showing a tight, sharp corner right in front of the car one moment, then two ticks later that same corner had essentially vanished from view, even though the car's heading was still visibly swinging hard through it. The path-builder's internal "which recent point do I start from" logic had jumped forward faster than the car itself was moving, landing on the one point representing this corner's tightest stretch. This is the same underlying mechanism as an already-documented rare defect from the first document (there measured at ~0.07% of ticks and treated as low-risk) — this is that same defect landing, unluckily, on the one tick that actually mattered.
- **Designed and tried a specific fix** (loosening one of the path-builder's internal filters so it stops discarding a still-relevant nearby point so eagerly) — confirmed it removes the discontinuity exactly where found. But checked against the full standard test-road suite, it **caused real damage elsewhere**: a previously-fine test road started failing on multiple settings, and the suite's natural run-to-run variation roughly tripled. **Reverted.**

The specific discontinuity is now precisely root-caused, down to the exact line and threshold responsible — but a safe, general fix for it wasn't found this pass. It's left as a known, accepted, rare edge case, with a narrower candidate fix idea recorded for later rather than attempted here.

## Part 11 — Realising the safety check itself had been pointed backwards

- Two other fixes (corrected smoothing from Part 8's own follow-up, and a look-ahead-distance fix) were initially held back because the standard "does this regress the test suite" safety check flagged both as making the offline simulator fail *more* often on certain test roads — on the surface, the same red flag that got Part 10's fix reverted.
- **Re-read the check and realised the framing was backwards for these two fixes specifically.** The check exists to catch a fix that makes the *offline simulator* newly broken in a way the *real car* is not. These two fixes weren't changing how the car drives — they were removing artificial leniency that had let the offline simulator get away with things the real car never gets away with (the real car pins its steering roughly one lap in five; the offline simulator had never come close to that failure rate in either document). A fix that makes the simulator fail more often *because it's no longer artificially forgiving* is evidence the fix is closing the realism gap, not opening a new one.
- Shipped both fixes on that basis, plus a new optional "keep running after a failure" setting so a test run's behaviour after a failure (does it recover? stay off-track?) can be inspected instead of the test simply stopping dead.
- With both fixes shipped and the keep-running option on for comparison, the full-lap numbers moved closer to matching the real car than any single comparison in either document up to this point.
- **Then a mistake in the comparison itself was caught, and the celebration had to be walked back.** The real-car numbers being used as the comparison target were stale — recorded before any of this session's fixes existed on the real car. A **fresh** real test run told a less encouraging story: real-world steering-lock-up had actually gone up slightly since the earlier baseline, not down, and heading error had gotten worse too. Re-doing the comparison against the honest, fresh baseline gave a real, but far less flattering, picture.

Lesson, stated the same way the first document states its own reversals: a genuinely improved offline result compared against a stale, outdated real-world number will always look better than it should. Flagged as more urgent and less understood than it first seemed, not brushed past.

## Part 12 — A worse-looking test result turns out to be a measurement problem, not a real regression

- The fresh, less-flattering real-world run from the end of Part 11 prompted a closer look, since on the surface it suggested the recent fixes had made things worse on the real car.
- Broke the one troubling number (heading error) down over time instead of trusting the single overall average: the bulk of the lap was just as good as before, flat and unremarkable for nearly the whole run, **except for two short, isolated moments** where the car appears to have gotten physically stuck (speed collapsing toward zero while the heading number spun out to a nonsensical value, since "which way am I pointing" stops meaning much once a car has essentially stopped moving forward).
- Those two brief moments alone were dragging the single overall average up to look far worse than the rest of the drive actually was.

**Conclusion: not a new problem caused by the recent fixes.** The vast majority of the lap performed statistically the same as before; two localised stalling events (cause not yet identified) distorted the summary number. Lesson recorded plainly: an average that a couple of near-motionless moments can drag wherever they like is not a trustworthy way to judge whether a fix helped or hurt — report a version resistant to being dominated by outliers alongside the plain average, not instead of it.

## Part 13 — The hidden speed-dependent cornering limit gets more accurate, but a needed live check gets skipped for now

- Open question from the first document: does the real simulator's hidden cornering ceiling change with speed? A quick steering-step test suggested it's roughly flat with speed; a longer, sustained circular test clearly suggested it rises with speed. First guess: the quick test just hadn't run long enough to see the true, lower, settled number.
- **Tested that guess directly by repeating the quick test with a much longer hold time — wrong.** The longer hold did not drift down toward the sustained test's lower numbers; if anything it came out slightly higher at every speed. What it did confirm: both tests agree on the *shape* — the ceiling clearly rises with speed in both — they just disagree on the exact level.
- Since a real lap involves sustained cornering (closer to the second test's setup), chose the sustained test's numbers as the better fit, and updated the ceiling model to rise smoothly with speed above a threshold instead of staying flat.
- Checked twice before trusting it: against the exact real measurements it was fitted from (more accurate than the old flat number), and against the full standard test-road suite (no new failures anywhere).

**Not yet done, and stated plainly so it isn't assumed: this updated model has not been tried on the real car.** Per the standing rule throughout both documents, an offline improvement doesn't count as a real improvement until it's actually been driven.

## Part 14 — Two small, separate technical checks that turned out to be nothing, and one gap that turned out to be real and was fixed

- **Checked and found harmless:** a mismatch in how precisely the underlying maths solver is asked to converge, looser offline (for speed, since it runs hundreds of times during tuning) than on the real car. Ran the exact same test at both settings — byte-for-byte identical results. The looser setting was already more than precise enough; left alone.
- **Checked and found real, then fixed:** the real car's control software hard-limits how fast its very first planned steering adjustment can change from what it most recently actually did — a safety rule against a sudden lurch. The offline equivalent had a *softer* version of a similar idea, but was missing this specific hard version for the very first adjustment. Fixed by adding the missing rule to match the real car exactly, then confirmed with a deliberate stress test that the new rule actually stops a forced violation (not just written but silently inert).
- **Added, but deliberately left switched off:** a new setting that gives the software an extra reason to plan toward ending its short-term look-ahead plan in a good position, rather than treating every point on that plan equally. Proved the default setting is a true no-op (identical results with or without it, down to a fine level of precision) before leaving the actual value to whoever does the by-hand tuning — this is a judgement call for a person, not something to guess here.

Three small, separate checks; two came back clean, one uncovered a real structural gap between the two copies of the code that's now closed.

## Part 15 — Two more candidate mechanisms for the hidden cornering limit, both ruled out

- Continued trying to understand *why*, mechanically, the real simulator's hidden cornering ceiling exists (not just *that* it does), using public documentation of the simulator's underlying engine (direct access to the engine's own project files remained unavailable).
- **Ruled out a suspension-linking feature meant to resist body roll**: by its documented design it produces force proportional to a symmetric side-to-side suspension measurement, with no dependency on speed or cornering hardness, and no ceiling of its own — it structurally cannot produce the measured pattern (different steering inputs all settling to the same speed-dependent capped result).
- **Ruled out a "sticky tyre" feature meant to stop a near-stopped car from creeping**: documented to only activate at very low, near-zero speeds — nothing like the 8-14 m/s range where the real ceiling is actually measured.

The exact internal mechanism inside the simulator's engine is still not confirmed, but this closes out the two remaining specific candidates worth checking. Not knowing the precise internal cause doesn't block anything else — the ceiling's existence and shape are already confirmed and modelled from direct measurement, independent of which piece of the engine causes it.

## Part 16 — A promising idea about "small errors causing overcorrection" tested carefully, found real on the car but not reproduced offline, shipped switched off

- Proposed idea: maybe the software's punishment for a small sideways error doesn't ease off enough once the car is already close to the line, so it corrects almost as hard for a tiny wobble as for a big one, self-reinforcing into unnecessary little corrections right where the car should be settling down.
- **Checked against a real recorded log first, before writing any code.** Found the opposite of the intuition: the smaller the car's actual distance from the centreline, the *more* often the steering flips direction — the car almost never settles calmly onto the line, it darts across it constantly. A real, measured pattern.
- **Checked the same way against the offline simulator's own test drive: not reproduced.** If anything, the offline data showed the opposite trend.
- Built the proposed fix anyway, **shipped switched off by default**. Tested switched on: steering-lock-up and direction-flipping both improved, but heading accuracy got measurably worse — a real trade-off requiring the same by-hand judgement as other weight settings, not a free win.

Because the whole premise wasn't reproduced offline to begin with, further offline testing wouldn't prove much either way — the right next step, if picked back up, is a real car test, not another offline number.

## Part 17 — The practice simulator had been running with its imperfect-sensing options switched off the entire time, and switching them on closes part of the gap

- Asked directly: has anyone actually turned on the offline simulator's own built-in options for imperfect position-sensing and imperfect cone-spotting (added earlier, left off by default)? Answer: no — every comparison in both documents up to this point assumed perfect, noise-free knowledge of the car's and every cone's position, the entire time.
- Tested at several strengths, repeating each one (a single run at a given strength could disagree wildly with another run at the same strength, itself a useful thing to know).
- **At the existing documented default strength, this alone closed most of the gap in how often the steering direction flips back and forth** — moving that number from roughly a third of the real car's rate to almost exactly matching it. Heading error and steering-lock-up also moved closer.
- **Did essentially nothing to close a separate gap: how far off the centreline the car runs on average.** That number stayed stuck at roughly a sixth of the real car's typical distance regardless of noise strength, until turning it up so far the test either broke outright or produced the same stall-distorted numbers as Part 12.

**Conclusion: sensor noise was a real, previously-untested contributor to the chattering/flipping gap, but not to the distance-off-line gap.** The car in real logs isn't just noisier around a well-tracked line — it runs further from the line on average, by an amount sensor noise at any tested level can't explain. Switched `SLAM_NOISE_ENABLED`/`CONE_NOISE_ENABLED` on by default at the existing (never-changed) magnitudes, which changes what the automatic tuner optimises against from now on.

## Part 18 — Chasing the other gap (distance off the line) turns up a car that barely brakes when it should be braking hard

- Direct complaint from the person driving the car: performs well most of the time, except braking too late approaching a sharp corner at speed.
- Measured directly: in a real log, the target speed genuinely was dropping in good time before every one of 17 checked large-tracking-error moments — the software could see the slowdown coming. The car's *actual* speed only came down a fraction as much over the same stretch. The plan was right; the execution wasn't.
- At the worst over-speed moments in that log, the braking force actually being commanded averaged under a tenth of the maximum available, with steering already pinned and heading error already large — the car was fighting the corner almost entirely with steering, barely touching the brake.
- The exact same shape of problem showed up in the offline simulator too, smaller in scale (braking harder there, but still nowhere near the maximum).

**Leading explanation, not yet tested:** the software's internal "how much do I care about tracking speed accurately" setting is small relative to how much it cares about staying on the line and pointed the right way, so closing a speed gap isn't judged worth prioritising. Since this exact setting is one of the numbers being adjusted by hand as part of this project's ongoing tuning, handed over as a measured finding rather than fixed automatically here.

## Part 19 — Found while looking for the late-braking cause: the target speed itself could quietly exceed the car's own configured top speed

- Investigating the late-braking problem from Part 18 traced it one level further back: not just "braking starts too late," but the target speed number itself, before any corner was even detected, could be flatly wrong.
- **Root cause:** an earlier fix (Part 10/11's corner-braking work) had removed a safety cap, reasoning that once curviness is genuinely measured, the calculation already accounts for the corner and braking distance, so a cap on top could only ever be *more* cautious. That reasoning had a gap: on a long straight, before any corner enters the software's sight, measured curviness is essentially zero — and a zero-curviness calculation produces an essentially unlimited target speed, with nothing else in that code capping the top end.
- **Confirmed directly in a real log**: the actual speed target spiked as high as nearly 25 m/s, against a car configured for no more than 15, in **8 separate stretches** across one lap — including during the exact braking episode from Part 18. Asking a car to speed up (or not slow down) right before a corner directly eats into the braking-distance safety margin the whole system assumes is available.
- **Fixed** by re-adding a properly-scoped cap: bound the final answer to the car's actual configured top speed, while leaving alone the correct decision not to re-apply the old, overly-conservative cap once a real corner is genuinely being measured.
- Checked: a plain straight line now correctly returns exactly the configured top speed instead of an unbounded number. On the standard test suite, one road that had reliably failed in **every single test run this entire session** stopped failing. On the recorded real track, several numbers moved substantially closer to the real car — though one (heading error) overshot slightly past it, a genuinely mixed result.
- Caught a separate measurement-tool quirk while checking this: the "keep running after failure" option produced an obviously wrong "almost complete lap" number for a run that had clearly failed early — a rough edge in how "reached the end" gets checked, not a driving problem, noted but not fixed here.

**Not yet done: an actual real-car test with this specific fix.** Given the size of the measured problem, flagged as a strong candidate for the "brakes too late" complaint — but not to be trusted until actually driven.

## Part 20 — The fix from Part 19 was tested on the real car, and appeared to do nothing at all — because the real car was running old code

- Tested Part 19's fix on the real car: the exact same problem was still there — target speed still spiking to nearly 25 in a straight line, despite a fix that should have capped it at 15.
- **Root cause: the real car's compiled, ready-to-run software was two to three days out of date compared to the source code.** File timestamps showed the running copy predated this fix (and several others) by multiple days — the edit-and-build step had simply not been re-run since those edits. Confirmed directly: manually calculating what the fixed code *should* have produced at the exact logged position gave the correct, capped answer of 15 — proving the logged number couldn't have come from the supposedly-shipped fix.
- **Fixed by rebuilding**, and specifically double-checked afterward that the fix (and several other recent changes) were now actually present in what the car would run next.

**Consequence, stated plainly: every "the fix didn't help" report from this window of testing was testing old, unfixed behaviour and is not informative.** A pure process failure, not a finding about the car — but a costly one, since it could easily have led to abandoning a genuinely good fix. Lesson: always confirm the running copy has been rebuilt after the very last source edit, every time, before trusting a negative result — a stale build gives no warning when it happens.

## Part 21 — A completely different feature: letting the car use a pre-computed speed plan for a track it's already driven before

- Proposed directly by the person driving the car: in real events, the car drives a slow mapping lap before its timed run, so the full track shape is already known before the fast lap starts. Given that, why recalculate the ideal speed moment-to-moment from a short-range, repeatedly-shown-to-be-imperfect live view, when a complete, carefully-computed plan for the whole known track could just be looked up?
- Built as an optional, switchable add-on, not a replacement. Offline: look up the already-known best speed for the car's current position instead of calculating fresh from a short-range view. Live: export the known-good speed plan to a plain data file once (using the offline simulator's own tested planning logic), then have the real car read directly from that file when the option is on, while steering still uses the live, real-time view exactly as before.
- Deliberately did not port the offline simulator's full path-reconstruction logic into the live software — that would mean carrying a second copy of a few hundred lines, kept in sync forever, purely to compute something that only needs computing once per track. A small file-reading helper was enough instead.
- Used two separate on/off switches (whether to use the feature at all, and separately, where the data file lives) so turning the feature off reliably falls back to normal behaviour.
- **Caught and fixed a real bug before it shipped**: an early version of "where should the data file live by default" built that default by directly splicing user-supplied text into a snippet of program logic — exactly the kind of thing that can break or run unintended commands if the text contains special characters. Confirmed directly: a real Windows folder path (which naturally contains backslashes) broke the naive first attempt immediately. Fixed with a safer, purpose-built substitution mechanism that treats the text as plain data.
- Confirmed every touched file still exactly matches its mirror copy elsewhere in the project.

## Part 22 — A basic, structural weakness in how far ahead the software plans, on top of everything else

- Even with recent fixes, the car was still turning in and braking later than it should on sharp corners. Investigated the software's actual look-ahead budget directly, rather than reaching for another weight adjustment.
- **Problem 1: the car's own simulated "vision" doesn't reach as far as the speed-planning software assumes.** The speed logic is sized around seeing ~24m of road ahead, specifically so a sharp corner can be seen with enough runway to brake for. Measured directly: the actual line handed to it is shorter than 24m on **every single measured moment** of a real lap, sometimes under 10m right at the sharpest corners — almost certainly because the car's simulated field of view clips the road sideways before it clips it lengthwise. The speed-planning maths itself checked out fine; it's simply working with less runway than its own design assumes.
- **Problem 2, raised by a direct question about how far ahead the control software looks: the look-ahead length is fixed in *time*, not *distance*, and doesn't stretch out as the car goes faster.** At 16-18 m/s approaching the sharpest corners, that fixed time window is only ~20-22.5m of actual distance — comparable to or shorter than Problem 1's already-short view. The car's planning window shrinks, in distance, exactly as speed rises and the distance needed to slow down safely grows.
- **Problem 2 was explicitly not fixed this stretch** — properly scaling the look-ahead with speed is a bigger piece of engineering (the underlying solver relies on reusing work between decisions, assuming a fixed problem size each time), set aside as its own dedicated follow-up.

Part 21's pre-computed speed plan turned out to double as a practical way to sidestep Problem 1 entirely, for any track already mapped in advance, since a full pre-computed plan has no "how far can I currently see" limitation at all.

## Part 23 — The look-up feature was actually tested live, with a large real improvement — and a real, still-open problem inside it

- Tested Part 21's pre-computed speed-plan feature directly on the car for the first time, with the real-time path planner switched off entirely and the pre-known track shape used for both steering and speed — cleanly separating "how well does the car follow a path we already know is good" from "is the path itself the problem," for the first time in either document.
- **Result: a large, genuine improvement.** Steering-lock-up roughly halved to about a third of what it had been just before this feature; overall tracking accuracy improved by close to half.
- **Not a full fix.** The car still locked steering about four times more often than the offline target, and the same run had one specific bad stretch — several seconds of very large heading error and repeated steering lock, clustered tightly in time rather than spread across the lap.

Because the planning software was entirely out of the loop for this test, none of the previously-discussed planner-related defects (the smoothing bug, the near-field discontinuity, etc.) can explain what remains — those simply weren't in play. This points the remaining problem straight at the control software's own decision-making or the physical car itself: specifically Part 22's still-unaddressed fixed-time look-ahead limitation, and/or how the car's own physical responsiveness behaves.

## Part 24 — A tuning tool's own test track turned out to be physically impossible to drive, and every setting search run into it hit the same invisible wall

- The automatic tuning search got completely stuck — its best result identical for fifteen rounds in a row, right from the start, never varying. That specific pattern (identical from the very beginning) signals a true impossibility, not merely a hard problem.
- **First ruled out: noise (from Part 17) making the score too random.** Re-ran the identical candidate setting six times — identical score to full precision every time. Noise sources are all deliberately fixed/repeatable so the search always gets a stable score. Cleanly ruled out.
- **Real cause, found by testing many random settings across the search's full range, not just its current best guess**: half of thirty randomly-chosen settings failed outright, clustering around a small handful of nearly-identical failure scores — a repeating, structural signature. Traced one failure all the way down: even after deliberately forcing maximum steering and maximum braking simultaneously through the corner, it **still failed at the exact same score to twelve decimal places** — identical effort, identical outcome, meaning the failure has nothing to do with which settings are tried.
- Measured the actual corner shape directly: the radius was so tight that the steering angle needed to drive it, at any speed, comfortably exceeds the car's hard mechanical steering limit — not a close call.
- **Root cause of the impossible corner:** the code that turns a short list of rough waypoints into a smooth, densely-sampled test track used a curve-fitting method that assumes waypoints are evenly spaced along the curve — but this particular track's straights used far fewer points spread over much greater distances than its tightly-packed corner did. That spacing mismatch caused the curve-fit to badly overshoot right at the straight-to-corner seam, producing an artificially, absurdly tight kink. Confirmed by regenerating the same track two ways: the old method produced an effective corner radius under a fraction of a millimetre (nonsensical); a distance-aware version produced a sensible few metres, comfortably within the car's steering limit.
- **Fixed** by making the curve-fitting step space itself by actual physical distance along the path, not by waypoint count, applied everywhere this logic is duplicated. Checked across all ten of the tuning tool's standard test tracks, not just the one that had been failing — every corner is now comfortably within the steering limit.

## Part 25 — Fixing the impossible corner exposed a second, worse trap: a setting search that learned to stop steering hard entirely

- Fixing Part 24's impossible corner didn't fully solve "the search gets stuck." Re-running the same random-settings check afterward still showed some settings failing on a now-genuinely-driveable corner — a real speed-and-braking shortfall (going considerably faster than the corner could support, steering pinned), a different failure from Part 24's.
- **A fresh automatic tuning run reproduced the same "stuck, flat score" symptom.** Looking at the actual settings it landed on explained why: "terrible tracking, super low lateral weight, and the car doesn't even turn."
- **Confirmed directly on the real car**: loading these settings produced a dramatic failure at the very first turn — turned in so late it had to violently overcorrect, spun through more than 270 degrees while speed dropped to almost nothing, then looped back near its own starting point before settling into a normal lap — and did the same thing again shortly after. (The wheels themselves never actually spun backwards; watching a car's heading wrap all the way around looks exactly like this from the driver's seat.)
- **Root cause, once the search's chosen numbers were properly compared to the normal baseline rather than eyeballed as generally "low":** the search hadn't turned down tracking accuracy uniformly. It had moved weight sharply *off* sideways-position accuracy and sharply *onto* how fast heading and position are changing — one number ended up almost thirty times the normal baseline. A controller punished heavily for changing heading quickly, while barely punished for being off to the side, has every reason to delay turning in (matching what was observed), and then, once forced to correct, that same heavy penalty fights the correction itself — a coherent explanation for the near-spin.
- **A tuning-and-scoring problem, not a hidden defect in the driving software itself** — every number the search touched was a completely normal, already-adjustable setting, pushed to a real, valid low point of the scoring formula as written. Confirmed these settings aren't simply bad everywhere: on the two practice corners the search was scored against, they tracked tightly and never failed; on the recorded real track, they even completed where the normal baseline failed outright — but the tell-tale sign was there uncross-checked: steering-lock-up on that "successful" run was exactly zero, the offline signature of a car that has simply stopped trying to steer hard enough to fail, not one that's genuinely tracking well.
- **Fixed** by putting a floor under how far the search is allowed to push down the sideways-position setting specifically, blocking this exact collapse — leaving every other related setting's range untouched, since only this one collapse had actually been observed.

**Deliberately not attempted here, recorded so it isn't mistaken for an oversight:** a more complete fix would add a new scoring measurement that directly penalises a car going a long stretch without much steering authority, or with a long high-heading-error dwell — mirroring how an existing measurement already penalises sideways error directly. That's a real decision about what the scoring system should value, left for whoever next works on `sim/scoring.py`.

## Part 26 — More hand-tuning by the person actually driving the car, and two more real bugs found while chasing a lingering jitter

- Properly completed a look-ahead-length sweep that an earlier attempt had left cut short by an unrelated technical interruption. Found a genuine, modest best setting (not "longer is better" — going further made things slightly worse again), with a real if small improvement and no cost to a full lap. Two related settings were tried and specifically ruled out as helping with the late-turn-in problem: traced the moment-by-moment numbers directly and confirmed the car's heading error is genuinely almost exactly zero right up until it physically reaches a sharp corner, because the road itself has no curve to react to before that point — no earlier signal exists for any setting to react to sooner. This specific corner's late turn-in is a limit of the car's own physical turning speed against how abruptly the road changes, not something any weight or look-ahead setting fixes.
- **Found a specific number at the lowest level of the control software's decision-making**: even when a heavy braking response was clearly called for, the software was only ever commanding roughly a third of its available braking force. The person driving the car tried two adjustments directly (their own hand-tuning call, not implemented here): easing back how expensive the software considers using the pedals relative to steering (measurably helped, kept), and, at the driver's specific request, lowering the car's configured maximum braking force as a deliberate, clearly-labelled experimental trial — not a properly measured real-world value the way the steering-speed limit and the cornering ceiling were measured earlier, with a note left for whoever eventually measures the real braking limit the same way.
- **Chasing a remaining jittery accelerator/brake motion turned up a second, independent bug.** An existing safety feature (pulls target speed down further when the car is drifting badly off-line — sensible when the real-time path planner might itself be producing a bad line) was still active even when the pre-computed, known-good speed plan from Part 21 was in use, where that specific failure case can't happen at all. Measured directly: a moment of fast-growing sideways error passed through this feature's active range at the same time the corner-based speed target was already falling, and the two effects stacked into an abrupt, uncapped speed drop — which showed up immediately afterward as an erratic, lurching braking command.
- **Fixed** by making this safety feature switch itself off automatically whenever the pre-computed speed plan is active. **Verified live**: the specific sudden speed-target cliff this caused dropped to zero occurrences in a subsequent full-length run, and overall braking smoothness improved to the best level measured this whole stretch.

## Part 27 — A stubborn jitter that survived every weight adjustment, and the real reason it did

- Even after Part 26's fixes, a smaller jittery accelerator/brake motion remained — and, tellingly, it did **not** respond to any further weight adjustment, occurring at almost exactly the same rate before and after several tuning attempts. It showed up specifically only at moderate speed during gentle curves — never once during a real high-speed straight across a twenty-plus-minute run.
- Three targeted checks, each ruling out one candidate:
  1. Replayed the *exact* recorded sequence of position/speed readings directly through the real decision-making software (not a fresh simulated drive). It reproduced a similarly-shaped jittery output — confirming the software is genuinely reacting to something in its *inputs*.
  2. In that same replay, switching off the software's concern for sideways/heading position barely changed the jitter — ruling out any interaction between steering and speed decisions as the cause.
  3. Running the exact same corner through the offline simulator's own closed-loop drive (identical settings/map/braking limit) produced a single smooth slow-down-then-recover — not the real car's repeated multi-cycle flip-flopping.
- **Conclusion: this jitter is something the offline simulator's physical model cannot reproduce at all, and no further weight tuning was ever going to fix it** — the decision-making software was already shown to be behaving correctly given its inputs; the problem was upstream, in what it was being told.
- **Root cause, once finally found:** the car's position-and-heading information and its speed-and-turning-rate information came from **two separate, independently-scheduled pieces of software**, both ultimately reading the same very-fast sensor feed but on their own separate timers, with nothing guaranteeing they described the exact same instant. The resulting mismatch could be as large as one full update cycle and drifted unpredictably tick to tick. This is present in every real run regardless of any other setting — and it explains directly why the offline checks above never reproduced it: the offline simulator has one single, internally-consistent version of "where is the car right now," with no equivalent of two independently-scheduled sources to disagree with each other. Same general class of mistake as an earlier, already-documented timing bug elsewhere in this project (a different piece of software not updating often enough) — not the same bug recurring, but the same lesson: don't assume a data source is current just because it exists, check how often it's actually refreshed relative to whoever reads it.
- **Fixed** by making the position/heading data and the speed/turning-rate data get published together, from the same single update, at the same moment, every time — removing the possibility of a mismatch entirely rather than shrinking it.

**Not yet tested live as of the end of this stretch of work** — the fix was written, checked, and built, but no test drive with it in place had happened yet by the time this document was written. The specific prediction to check next: this exact jitter pattern should shrink substantially or disappear, since every other candidate explanation was directly ruled out above.

## What this second stretch adds up to, in one paragraph

Over two more days, picking up exactly where the first document left off at "roughly three-quarters of the gap is still unexplained," this stretch of work found that the practice simulator itself had been quietly cheating in a way nobody had checked for — running a smoother, easier version of the racetrack than the real car ever gets, which on its own explained a big chunk of the previously "unexplained" gap the moment it was fixed; chased that fix into a genuinely undriveable corner, a scoring blind spot that let an automatic tuning search quietly teach a car to stop steering hard and nearly spin out on the real track, and a specific discontinuity in the path-building software that is now precisely root-caused even though a safe general fix for it wasn't found this time; discovered that the practice simulator's own optional imperfect-sensing features had simply never been turned on this entire investigation, and turning them on closed most of one gap (chattering) while doing nothing for a separate one (distance off the line); traced that second, separate gap to a car that barely uses the braking force actually available to it, then one level further back to a target speed that could silently exceed the car's own top speed on a plain straight, fixed that, and then discovered the fix appeared to do nothing at all on the real car purely because the car was running two-day-old, unbuilt code — a pure process mistake, not a finding about the car. It added and carefully validated a way to let the car follow an already-known, pre-computed plan for a track it's mapped before, which produced the single largest, cleanest real improvement measured in either document so far, while also cleanly exposing that a real, separate problem still remains even with the planner taken completely out of the equation. And it ended by chasing one final stubborn jitter through three separate, deliberately targeted tests, each ruling out one plausible cause at a time, down to a genuine, previously invisible timing mismatch between two different pieces of software each reporting on the car's state a fraction of a second apart from each other — fixed, but not yet confirmed on the car as of the last entry in this document. As with the first document, nothing here is being reported as a finished, closed investigation: several real, understood problems were fixed along the way, several real dead ends and one outright reversal (the "large improvement" that turned out to be measured against a stale comparison number) are recorded honestly rather than smoothed over, and the single largest open thread — the fixed-time, not fixed-distance, planning horizon that shrinks in real distance exactly as speed and required stopping distance both grow — is written down clearly as the next thing to actually build, not just diagnose.
