## Stage 8 — 2026-08-07: the offline harness was testing a different track than it thought

**Plain-language summary:** Nobody had checked whether the offline test
harness calls the shared path-building logic with the *same tuning numbers*
the real car uses — only whether the underlying code files were identical
(they were). They weren't the same numbers. Fixing this produced the single
largest improvement of either log.

**What was found and fixed:**

- The real car passes four specific tuning values into the path-building
  step (smoothing amount, look-ahead distance, two more). The offline
  harness's equivalent call passed none of them, silently falling back to
  generic defaults. Two of four happened to match by coincidence; two
  didn't — most importantly, the offline harness was smoothing the
  centreline **over 3× more heavily** than the real car's tuned setting.
- Fixed by passing the same four values through explicitly.

**Effect:** offline steering-lock-up on the recorded test track more than
doubled (under 5% → over 10%), and a previously-clean run now failed
partway through. The "unexplained gap" narrowed from ~17.5 percentage points
to ~11 — the single biggest reduction in either log, larger than every
previous fix combined.

## Stage 10 — 2026-08-07: a corner that couldn't be driven

**Plain-language summary:** A lap kept failing at almost the same point even
after Stage 8/9's fixes. The cause was a discontinuous jump in the
centreline right where the car needed it most — a fix was found but caused
regressions elsewhere, so it was reverted.

**What was found:**

- Re-measured after Stage 8 + the concurrent braking-distance fix: **same
  failure point, unmoved.**
- First theory — the tracking-error speed gate (Stage 4) cutting in too
  aggressively — **ruled out directly**: removing it made the failure worse
  and broke a previously-fine road. Reinstated.
- Real cause: the centreline **jumped discontinuously** — a tight corner
  visible one tick, essentially gone two ticks later, despite the car's
  heading still swinging hard through it. The path-builder's "which recent
  point to start from" logic had jumped ahead faster than the car itself
  moved, skipping the corner's only near-field representative point. Same
  underlying mechanism as an already-documented rare defect from the first
  log (there measured at ~0.07% of ticks, treated as low-risk) — this is
  that defect landing on the one tick that mattered.
- **Attempted fix** (loosen the filter discarding the near-field point):
  confirmed it removes the discontinuity, but caused a previously-fine road
  to start failing and roughly tripled the test suite's run-to-run variance.
  **Reverted.**

Root-caused down to the exact line and threshold; no safe general fix found
this pass. Left as a known, accepted rare edge case with a narrower
candidate fix idea recorded for later.

## Stage 11 — 2026-08-08: the safety check was being read backwards

**Plain-language summary:** Two fixes were being held back by the standard
"does this regress the test suite" check, which flagged them as making the
offline simulator fail *more often*. Re-reading the check's actual purpose
showed that was the wrong red flag for these two specifically — then a
follow-up caught a stale comparison baseline that undercut the celebration.

**What happened:**

- Two fixes (Stage 8's own smoothing follow-up, a look-ahead-distance fix)
  were held back because the regression check flagged both as making
  offline fail more often on certain synthetic roads — superficially the
  same red flag that reverted Stage 10's fix.
- **Re-read the check's purpose and found the framing backwards for these
  two.** The check exists to catch a fix that makes the offline simulator
  newly broken in a way the real car is not. These two fixes weren't
  changing how the car drives — they were removing artificial leniency
  (the real car pins steering ~1 lap in 5; offline had never come close in
  either log). A fix that makes the simulator fail more often *because it's
  no longer artificially forgiving* is evidence of closing the realism gap,
  not opening one.
- Shipped both, plus a new `continue_after_dnf` option so post-failure
  behaviour (recovers? stays off-track?) can be inspected instead of the run
  simply stopping.
- With both shipped, full-lap numbers moved closer to the real car than any
  comparison in either log up to this point.
- **Then caught a mistake in the comparison itself.** The real-car baseline
  being used predated all of this session's fixes. A fresh real run told a
  less encouraging story: real-world lock-up had gone up slightly, not down,
  and heading error had gotten worse too.

> **Lesson:** an improved offline result compared against a stale real-world
> number will always look better than it should. Flagged as more urgent and
> less understood than it first seemed.

**What was found:**

- Broke the one troubling number (heading error) down over time instead of

  unremarkable, **except for two short isolated moments** where the car

## Stage 14 — 2026-08-08: two small checks, one real gap closed

**Plain-language summary:** Three unrelated technical checks. Two came back
clean; one uncovered a real structural gap between the offline and live
copies, now closed.

**What happened:**

- **Solver tolerance (checked, harmless):** offline uses a looser
  convergence tolerance than live (for tuning speed). Same test at both
  settings gave byte-identical results — already precise enough.
- **First-tick slew-rate limit (checked, real, fixed):** the live controller
  hard-limits how fast its *very first* planned steering adjustment can
  change from the last actual command. Offline had a softer general version
  of a similar idea but was missing this specific hard first-tick rule.
  Fixed to match; confirmed with a deliberate stress test that the rule
  actually stops a forced violation.
- **Terminal cost weight (added, deliberately off):** a new setting that
  gives extra weight to ending the short-term plan in a good position.
  Proved the default is a true no-op (identical results with/without, to
  fine precision) before leaving the actual value to hand-tuning.

## Stage 15 — 2026-08-08: two more ceiling mechanisms ruled out

**Plain-language summary:** Continued investigating *why*, mechanically, the
FSDS cornering ceiling exists — using public engine documentation (direct
access to the engine project files remained unavailable). Two more candidate
mechanisms ruled out.

**What was ruled out:**

- **Anti-roll-bar (body-roll resistance):** by documented design, produces
  force proportional to a symmetric suspension measurement with no
  dependency on speed or cornering hardness, and no ceiling of its own —
  structurally cannot produce the measured pattern.
- **Sticky-tyre / anti-creep friction:** documented to activate only at
  near-zero speed — nothing like the 8–14 m/s range where the real ceiling
  is measured.

The exact internal engine mechanism is still unconfirmed, but this closes
the two remaining specific candidates. Not knowing the precise cause doesn't
block anything else — the ceiling's shape is already confirmed and modelled
from direct measurement, independent of which engine feature causes it.

## Stage 16 — 2026-08-08: a promising idea that didn't reproduce offline

**Plain-language summary:** Hypothesis: the software doesn't ease off its
correction for small tracking errors enough, causing self-reinforcing
overcorrection right where the car should settle calmly onto the line.
Confirmed real on the live car, not reproduced offline — shipped disabled.

**What happened:**

- **Checked against a real log first, before writing code.** Found the
  opposite of the intuition: the *smaller* the car's actual distance from
  centreline, the *more* often steering flips direction — the car almost
  never settles, it darts across the line constantly. A real, measured
  pattern.
- **Checked the same way offline: not reproduced.** If anything, offline
  showed the opposite trend.
- Built the fix anyway, **shipped disabled by default**. Tested enabled:
  lock-up and direction-flipping both improved, but heading accuracy got
  measurably worse — a real trade-off needing the same hand-tuning judgement
  as other weights, not a free win.

Since the premise wasn't reproduced offline to begin with, further offline
testing wouldn't prove much — the right next step, if revisited, is a real
car test.

## Stage 17 — 2026-08-08: sensor noise was off the whole time

**Plain-language summary:** Every comparison in both logs up to this point
had assumed perfect, noise-free position and cone-sensing offline. Turning
on the simulator's own existing (never-used) noise options closed most of
the reversal-rate gap — but none of the distance-off-line gap.

**What happened:**

- Asked directly: has anyone turned on `SLAM_NOISE_ENABLED`/
  `CONE_NOISE_ENABLED`, added earlier but always left off? Answer: no.
- Tested at several strengths, each repeated (a single run at a given
  strength could disagree wildly with another run at the same strength).
- **At the existing documented default strength, closed most of the
  reversal-rate gap** — from roughly ⅓ of the real car's rate to almost an
  exact match. Heading error and lock-up also moved closer.
- **Did essentially nothing for the separate distance-off-line gap** —
  stayed at roughly ⅙ of the real car's typical distance regardless of
  noise strength, until pushed so high the test broke outright or produced
  the same stall-distorted numbers as Stage 12.

> **Conclusion:** sensor noise was a real, previously-untested contributor
> to the chattering gap, but not the distance-off-line gap — the real car
> runs further from the line on average by an amount no tested noise level
> explains. Switched both noise options on by default at their existing
> magnitudes, changing what the automatic tuner optimises against from now
> on.

## Stage 18 — 2026-08-08: the car barely brakes when it should brake hard

**Plain-language summary:** Direct complaint from the driver: performs well
except braking too late into sharp corners. Measured directly — the plan was
right, the execution wasn't. The car used under 10% of available braking
force even with steering already pinned and heading error already large.

**What was measured:**

- In a real log, target speed genuinely dropped in good time before every
  one of 17 checked large-tracking-error moments — the software saw the
  slowdown coming.
- Actual speed only came down a fraction as much over the same stretch.
- At the worst over-speed moments, commanded braking force averaged under
  10% of maximum, with steering already pinned and heading error already
  large — the car was fighting the corner almost entirely with steering.
- Same shape of problem showed up offline too, smaller in scale.

**Leading explanation, not yet tested:** the speed-tracking cost weight is
small relative to the lateral/heading cost, so closing a speed gap isn't
judged worth prioritising. Handed over as a measured finding for the
project's ongoing hand-tuning rather than fixed here.

## Stage 19 — 2026-08-08: target speed could exceed the car's own top speed

**Plain-language summary:** Investigating Stage 18's late braking traced one
level further back: the target speed itself, before any corner was even
detected, could be flatly wrong — spiking above the car's own configured top
speed on a plain straight.

**What was found and fixed:**

- **Root cause:** Stage 10/11's corner-braking work had removed a safety cap
  on the reasoning that once curviness is genuinely measured, braking
  distance is already accounted for, so a cap on top could only ever be
  *more* cautious. Gap in that reasoning: on a straight, before any corner is
  in sight, measured curviness is ~zero — and a zero-curviness calculation
  produces an unlimited target speed, with nothing else capping the top end.
- **Confirmed in a real log:** actual speed target spiked to nearly 25 m/s
  against a car configured for no more than 15, in 8 separate stretches
  across one lap — including during the exact braking episode from Stage 18.
- **Fixed** by re-adding a properly-scoped cap: bound the final answer to the
  car's configured top speed, while leaving the corner-measurement logic
  alone once a real corner is genuinely detected.
- Checked: a plain straight now correctly returns exactly the configured top
  speed. One road that had failed in **every single test run this entire
  session** stopped failing. On the recorded real track, several numbers
  moved substantially closer to the real car (heading error overshot
  slightly past it — a genuinely mixed result).
- Caught a separate tooling quirk: `continue_after_dnf` produced an obviously
  wrong "almost complete lap" number for an early failure — noted, not fixed
  here.

> **Not yet done:** an actual real-car test of this fix. Flagged as a strong
> candidate for the "brakes too late" complaint but not to be trusted until
> driven.

## Stage 20 — 2026-08-09: the Stage 19 fix appeared to do nothing live

**Plain-language summary:** Tested Stage 19's fix on the real car — the same
problem was still there. Cause: the real car was running a stale build.

**What was found and fixed:**

- Same problem observed live: target speed still spiked to nearly 25 in a
  straight line, despite the fix.
- **Root cause: the live car's compiled software was 2–3 days out of date**
  compared to source. File timestamps predated this fix (and several others)
  by multiple days — the build step hadn't been re-run since those edits.
  Confirmed directly: hand-calculating what the fixed code *should* have
  produced at the exact logged position gave the correct, capped answer of
  15 — the logged number couldn't have come from the supposedly-shipped fix.
- **Fixed by rebuilding**, and specifically re-verified the fix (and several
  other recent changes) were now actually present in the next run.

> **Consequence:** every "the fix didn't help" report from this window was
> testing old, unfixed behaviour and is not informative — a process failure,
> not a finding about the car, but a costly one. Always confirm the running
> copy has been rebuilt after the last source edit before trusting a
> negative result.

## Stage 21 — 2026-08-09: precomputed speed plan for a known track

**Plain-language summary:** Driver-proposed feature: in real events, the car
drives a slow mapping lap before its timed run, so the full track is already
known in advance. Why recalculate speed moment-to-moment from a
short-range, imperfect live view when a complete precomputed plan for the
whole track could be looked up instead?

**What was built:**

- Optional, switchable add-on, not a replacement. Offline: look up the
  known-best speed for the car's current position instead of calculating
  fresh from a short-range view. Live: export the plan to a data file once
  (using the offline simulator's own tested logic), then have the real car
  read it directly when the option is on — steering still uses the live
  real-time view exactly as before.
- Deliberately did not port the offline path-reconstruction logic into live
  code (would mean carrying a second copy of a few hundred lines, kept in
  sync forever, to compute something needed only once per track). A small
  file-reading helper was enough.
- Two separate on/off switches (use the feature at all; where the data file
  lives) so disabling reliably falls back to normal behaviour.
- **Caught and fixed a real bug before shipping:** an early version of "where
  does the data file live by default" built that default by directly
  splicing user-supplied text into program logic — a shell-injection-style
  risk. Confirmed directly: a real Windows path (containing backslashes)
  broke the naive first attempt immediately. Fixed with a proper
  parameterized substitution instead of string splicing.
- Confirmed every touched file still exactly matches its mirror elsewhere in
  the project.

## Stage 22 — 2026-08-09: the look-ahead window is structurally too short

**Plain-language summary:** Even with recent fixes, the car still turned in
and braked later than it should on sharp corners. Investigated the actual
look-ahead budget directly rather than reaching for another weight
adjustment — found two separate structural shortfalls.

**What was found:**

- **Problem 1 — field of view clips before 24 m.** The speed logic is sized
  around seeing ~24 m of road ahead (enough runway to brake for a sharp
  corner). Measured: the actual line handed to it was shorter than 24 m on
  **every single measured moment** of a real lap, sometimes under 10 m right
  at the sharpest corners — almost certainly because the simulated field of
  view clips the road sideways before it clips it lengthwise.
- **Problem 2 — the horizon is fixed in time, not distance.** At 16–18 m/s
  approaching the sharpest corners, a fixed-time window is only ~20–22.5 m
  of actual distance — comparable to or shorter than Problem 1's already-short
  view. The planning window shrinks in distance exactly as speed rises and
  the distance needed to slow down safely grows.
- **Problem 2 deliberately not fixed this stretch** — properly scaling
  look-ahead with speed is a bigger piece of engineering (the solver relies
  on reusing work between decisions, assuming a fixed problem size). Set
  aside as its own follow-up.

Stage 21's precomputed plan turned out to double as a practical way to
sidestep Problem 1 for any already-mapped track, since a full precomputed
plan has no "how far can I currently see" limitation.

## Stage 23 — 2026-08-09: the precomputed plan tested live — a real improvement

**Plain-language summary:** Tested Stage 21's feature directly on the car for
the first time, with the real-time path planner switched off entirely. Large
genuine improvement — but not a full fix.

**What was found:**

- With the pre-known track shape used for both steering and speed, this
  cleanly separated "how well does the car follow a path already known to
  be good" from "is the path itself the problem," for the first time in
  either log.
- **Result: a large, genuine improvement.** Steering-lock-up roughly halved
  to about a third of what it had been just before this feature; overall
  tracking accuracy improved by close to half.
- **Not a full fix.** The car still locked steering ~4× more often than the
  offline target, and the run had one specific bad stretch — several
  seconds of very large heading error and repeated lock, tightly clustered
  in time.

Because the planner was entirely out of the loop, none of the
previously-discussed planner defects (smoothing bug, near-field
discontinuity, etc.) can explain what remains. This points the residual
problem at the controller's own decision-making or the physical car itself —
specifically Stage 22's unaddressed fixed-time horizon, and/or the car's own
physical responsiveness.

## Stage 24 — 2026-08-09: a tuning test track was physically undriveable

**Plain-language summary:** The automatic tuning search got completely
stuck — identical best result for 15 rounds straight, from the very start.
Traced to a synthetic test corner with an impossible steering-angle
requirement, caused by a curve-fitting bug that assumed evenly-spaced
waypoints.

**What was found and fixed:**

- Identical-from-the-start is a signature of true impossibility, not just a
  hard problem.
- **Ruled out noise first:** re-ran an identical candidate six times —
  identical score to full precision every time. All noise sources are
  deliberately fixed/repeatable for the search. Cleanly ruled out.
- **Real cause, found by testing random settings across the search's full
  range:** half of 30 randomly-chosen settings failed outright, clustering
  around nearly-identical failure scores — a structural signature. One
  failure traced all the way down: even with maximum steering and maximum
  braking forced simultaneously, it **still failed at the exact same score
  to twelve decimal places** — meaning the failure had nothing to do with
  which settings were tried.
- Measured the corner directly: the radius was so tight that the required
  steering angle, at any speed, comfortably exceeded the car's hard
  mechanical limit.
- **Root cause:** the code turning rough waypoints into a smooth,
  densely-sampled test track assumed even spacing along the curve — but this
  track's straights used far fewer points spread over much greater
  distances than its tightly-packed corner. The mismatch caused the
  curve-fit to badly overshoot at the straight-to-corner seam. Confirmed by
  regenerating the same track two ways: the old method produced an effective
  radius under a fraction of a millimetre (nonsensical); a distance-aware
  version produced a sensible few metres, comfortably within the steering
  limit.
- **Fixed** by making the curve-fit space itself by physical distance along
  the path, not by waypoint count, applied everywhere this logic is
  duplicated. Checked across all 10 standard test tracks — every corner now
  comfortably within the steering limit.

## Stage 25 — 2026-08-09: tuning learned to stop steering

**Plain-language summary:** Fixing Stage 24's impossible corner didn't fully
solve "the search gets stuck." A fresh tuning run reproduced the same
symptom on a now-genuinely-driveable corner — the search had learned that
barely steering at all scores well, confirmed live as a near-spin.

**What was found and fixed:**

- Re-running the random-settings check afterward still showed some settings
  failing on the now-driveable corner — a different failure this time (going
  considerably faster than the corner could support, steering pinned).
- **A fresh automatic tuning run reproduced the same "stuck, flat score"
  symptom.** The settings it landed on explained why: "terrible tracking,
  super low lateral weight, and the car doesn't even turn."
- **Confirmed directly on the real car**: loading these settings produced a
  dramatic failure at the very first turn — turned in so late it violently
  overcorrected, spun through 270°+ while speed dropped to near zero, looped
  back near its own starting point, then did it again shortly after. (The
  wheels never actually spun backwards — a heading wraparound looks exactly
  like this from the driver's seat.)
- **Root cause, once the search's numbers were compared to baseline rather
  than eyeballed as generally "low":** the search hadn't turned down
  tracking accuracy uniformly. It had moved weight sharply *off*
  sideways-position accuracy and sharply *onto* heading/position rate — one
  number ended up ~30× the normal baseline. A controller punished heavily
  for changing heading quickly, barely punished for being off to the side,
  has every reason to delay turning in — and then, once forced to correct,
  the same heavy penalty fights the correction itself.
- **A tuning-and-scoring problem, not a hidden defect** — every touched
  number was a completely normal, already-adjustable setting pushed to a
  real, valid low point of the scoring formula as written. These settings
  weren't simply bad everywhere: on the search's own two practice corners
  they tracked tightly and never failed; on the recorded real track they
  even completed where the normal baseline failed outright — but
  steering-lock-up on that "successful" run was exactly zero, the offline
  signature of a car that has simply stopped trying to steer hard enough to
  fail, not one that's genuinely tracking well.
- **Fixed** by putting a floor under how far the search is allowed to push
  down the sideways-position weight specifically — leaving every other
  related setting's range untouched, since only this one collapse had
  actually been observed.

> **Deliberately not attempted, recorded so it isn't mistaken for an
> oversight:** a more complete fix would add a scoring measurement that
> directly penalises a long stretch without much steering authority, or a
> long high-heading-error dwell — mirroring how sideways error is already
> penalised directly. Left as a decision for whoever next works on
> `sim/scoring.py`.

## Stage 26 — 2026-08-09: hand-tuning, plus two more real bugs

**Plain-language summary:** The driver did hand-tuning on look-ahead length
and braking cost. Chasing a lingering jitter turned up an independent bug: a
safety gate stacking with the precomputed speed plan to produce lurching
braking.

**What happened:**

- Completed a look-ahead-length sweep an earlier attempt had left cut short.
  Found a genuine, modest best setting (not "longer is better" — going
  further made things slightly worse again). Two related settings were
  tried and ruled out for the late-turn-in problem specifically: the car's
  heading error is genuinely almost exactly zero right up until it
  physically reaches a sharp corner, because the road itself has no curve to
  react to before that point — no earlier signal exists for any setting to
  react to sooner. This corner's late turn-in is a limit of the car's own
  physical turning speed against how abruptly the road changes, not
  something any weight or look-ahead setting fixes.
- Found a specific number at the lowest level of the controller: even when
  heavy braking was clearly called for, the software only ever commanded
  roughly ⅓ of available braking force. The driver made two hand-tuning
  calls (not implemented as measured fixes here): eased back the relative
  cost of pedal use vs. steering (measurably helped, kept), and lowered the
  configured max braking force as a deliberate, clearly-labelled
  experimental trial (not a properly measured real-world value the way the
  steering-speed limit and cornering ceiling were — noted for whoever
  eventually measures the real braking limit the same way).
- **Chasing a remaining jittery accelerator/brake motion turned up a second,
  independent bug.** The tracking-error speed gate (sensible when the
  real-time planner might produce a bad line) was still active even when
  the precomputed speed plan (Stage 21) was in use, where that specific
  failure mode can't happen. A moment of fast-growing sideways error passed
  through this gate's active range at the same time the corner-based speed
  target was already falling, and the two effects stacked into an abrupt,
  uncapped speed drop — showing up as an erratic, lurching braking command.
- **Fixed** by making the gate switch itself off automatically whenever the
  precomputed speed plan is active. **Verified live**: the specific
  speed-target cliff dropped to zero occurrences in a subsequent full-length
  run; overall braking smoothness reached the best level measured this
  stretch.

## Stage 27 — 2026-08-09: a jitter the offline simulator cannot reproduce

**Plain-language summary:** A smaller jitter survived every weight
adjustment and didn't respond to tuning at all — the tell that it wasn't a
tuning problem. Root cause: two separately-scheduled pieces of software
disagreeing about the car's exact current state.

**What was found and fixed:**

- Even after Stage 26, a smaller jittery accelerator/brake motion remained,
  occurring at almost exactly the same rate before and after several tuning
  attempts. Showed up specifically at moderate speed during gentle curves —
  never once during a high-speed straight across a 20+ minute run.
- Three targeted checks, each ruling out one candidate:
  1. Replayed the *exact* recorded sequence of position/speed readings
     directly through the live decision-making software (not a fresh
     simulated drive) — reproduced a similarly-shaped jittery output,
     confirming the software is genuinely reacting to something in its
     *inputs*.
  2. Switching off the software's concern for sideways/heading position in
     that same replay barely changed the jitter — ruling out any
     steering/speed-decision interaction.
  3. Running the exact same corner through the offline simulator's own
     closed-loop drive (identical settings/map/braking limit) produced a
     single smooth slow-down-then-recover — not the real car's repeated
     multi-cycle flip-flopping.
- **Conclusion: this jitter is something the offline simulator's physical
  model cannot reproduce at all, and no further weight tuning was ever going
  to fix it** — the software was already shown to be behaving correctly
  given its inputs; the problem was upstream, in what it was being told.
- **Root cause:** the car's position/heading and its speed/turning-rate
  information came from **two separately-scheduled pieces of software**,
  both ultimately reading the same fast sensor feed but on independent
  timers, with nothing guaranteeing they described the exact same instant.
  The mismatch could be as large as one full update cycle and drifted
  unpredictably tick to tick. Present in every real run regardless of any
  other setting — and explains directly why offline never reproduced it: the
  offline simulator has one single, internally-consistent "where is the car
  right now," with no equivalent of two disagreeing sources. Same general
  class of mistake as the Stage 3 pose-rate bug — not the same bug
  recurring, but the same lesson: don't assume a data source is current just
  because it exists; check how often it's actually refreshed relative to
  whoever reads it.
- **Fixed** by publishing position/heading and speed/turning-rate together,
  from the same single update, at the same moment, every time — removing the
  possibility of a mismatch entirely rather than shrinking it.

> **Not yet tested live as of the end of this stretch.** The fix was
> written, checked, and built, but no test drive with it in place had
> happened yet. Specific prediction to check next: this exact jitter pattern
> should shrink substantially or disappear, since every other candidate
> explanation was directly ruled out above.
