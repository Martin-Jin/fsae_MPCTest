# Changelog: August 4 to August 7 - the "why does the real car drive worse than the simulation" investigation

This document explains, in plain language, everything that was changed and learned in the `fsae_MPCTest` project between August 4 and August 7, 2026. This was a busy, connected stretch of work: 61 separate updates that all build on each other. Before this stretch there was a five-day gap with no changes, which is how the start of this series was identified. The document is written so that someone without a coding or robotics background can follow the story of what happened, why each step was taken, what was discovered, and what happens next.

Some quick background: this project is an offline "practice simulator" for tuning the self-driving software that controls a race car. The idea is that you can test and adjust the car's driving logic on a computer, safely and quickly, before trying it on the real car in the real simulator (called FSDS). For that to be useful, the practice simulator has to behave like the real thing. Most of this stretch of work is the story of discovering that it didn't behave like the real thing closely enough, and slowly figuring out why.

## Summary: what changed and why, in order

1. **Tidied up and re-synced the project.** Before making changes, the team made sure the practice simulator's copy of the driving logic matched the current "real" version, and reorganised the files so they're easier to work with and easier to eventually submit as an official update.
2. **Fixed some basic setup and file-path problems.** Small but important fixes so that recordings of test drives actually end up somewhere the tools can find them, and so the startup scripts pointed at the right files.
3. **Fixed five different bugs in "replaying a real track."** The team recorded a real competition track and tried to load it into the practice simulator to test against. It took five separate fixes (the replayed lap ran backwards, stopped too early, looped strangely, etc.) before a recorded lap could be loaded and replayed correctly at all.
4. **Improved the tools used for tuning.** Added some behind-the-scenes improvements to the automatic tuning process (the system that searches for good driving settings) so it works faster and keeps better records of what it tried.
5. **Found and fixed a cause of steering wobble.** The car's steering was reacting too aggressively to small errors, causing a shimmy. Rebalanced the settings so it corrects itself more smoothly, and improved how "steering wobble" is measured so the tuning process can actually detect and discourage it.
6. **Retuned the driving settings and expanded the practice-copy of the code.** After the wobble fix, the settings were re-tuned, and the "staging copy" of the driving code (kept ready for submitting to the main project) was expanded to include everything, not just part of it.
7. **Solved the real cause of steering "chatter" seen on the actual car.** This turned out to be three stacked problems: the practice simulator let the steering turn faster than the real car physically can, a logging mistake was hiding how often the steering was maxed out, and the car's position sensor was only updating half as often as the steering computer was checking it - causing it to "panic" and overcorrect. All three were fixed.
8. **Fixed the car speeding into corners it couldn't handle.** After the steering fix, a new problem appeared: the target speed didn't slow down enough when the car was already struggling to stay on the track. Added safety logic so the car eases off the speed when things are going wrong, while noting that this is a patch, not a permanent fix, for a deeper track-mapping issue described later.
9. **Fixed the "scoring system" used to judge how well the car drove.** The scoring system was accidentally ignoring 10 of the 12 things it was supposed to care about, so it was only really judging two things. Fixed the maths so all the things that matter actually count.
10. **Rebuilt how a "good lap" is scored, using a proper target lap time.** Replaced a rough guess at what a "good lap time" should be with an actual calculation of the fastest physically possible lap, so the scoring system has a fair target to measure against, and reorganised how crashes/going off-track are penalised so a car can't "buy its way out" of a crash by driving cleanly otherwise.
11. **Started comparing the practice simulator directly against the real car.** Added detailed logging to both and discovered the real car's position updates were sometimes freezing for up to a full second - a real difference the practice simulator didn't reproduce at all. Modelled that freezing behaviour, but discovered on its own it didn't explain the gap.
12. **Ruled out several possible causes one by one.** Carefully tested and eliminated "tyre grip," "general understeer," and a couple of other plausible explanations for why the real car behaves differently, using careful, repeatable experiments rather than guesses.
13. **Found the real cause: the real simulator secretly limits how hard the car can turn.** Through a dedicated experiment, discovered that above a certain speed, the real simulator (FSDS) invisibly caps how sharply the car can turn - something the practice simulator didn't know about at all.
14. **Built that limit into the practice simulator.** Added the discovered turning limit into the practice simulator's model of the car, and refined it twice after early versions either weren't strict enough or were too strict and made the practice car crash.
15. **Corrected a subtle mathematical mistake in how that limit was modelled.** The first version of the limit could never settle at the exact right value by design. Fixed the underlying maths, which made the model noticeably more accurate.
16. **Properly measured how much this explained - and found it wasn't the whole story.** Built better tools to test results over many different tracks instead of just one, and discovered this newly-found turning limit only explains at most a quarter of the difference between real and practice driving. Something else is still missing.
17. **Investigated whether the path-planning logic itself was jumping around.** Checked whether the way the software builds and smooths the car's intended path each moment was causing sudden, unrealistic swings in direction. Found this wasn't a significant factor on the specific track being tested.
18. **Fixed a real bug in how the car remembers cone positions, and closed a data gap.** Found and fixed a bug that could create duplicate "cone" (track boundary marker) entries. Also added a way to simulate imperfect cone-detection, since the practice simulator currently assumes perfect vision, which the real car doesn't have.
19. **Fixed the scripts that start up a real test, unblocking real test drives.** Fixed two bugs stopping the team from even running a live test on the real car/simulator setup this session, including a networking issue specific to how the test computer talks to the simulator.
20. **Got the first real side-by-side comparison of the real car and the practice simulator, and researched the simulator's inner workings.** Ran a real test and compared it to the practice simulator. Results looked better, but the test mixed together too many changes at once to know which one actually helped. Also researched whether the found turning limit might be a known, documented setting inside the simulator's game engine - plausible, but unconfirmed since the relevant file can't currently be opened.
21. **Corrected an overstated number, then ran a clean controlled test to isolate the real cause of the improvement.** Realised an earlier measurement of a path-planning glitch was much smaller than first reported. Ran a proper "on/off" experiment reintroducing an old bug on purpose, which confirmed a specific fix (how often the car's position is updated) really matters - but also revealed that results vary a lot between runs, and a single test isn't enough to draw firm conclusions. The stretch of work ends with the team explicitly noting this lesson for next time.

Each of these is explained in full below, in the order it happened, with the reasoning behind each step and what was learned from it.

---

## 1. Getting organised: syncing and reorganising the project

The practice simulator's driving logic had gotten out of date compared to the "official" version used on the real car. Before any new work could be trusted, the team pulled across the current official version of the path-planning and driving logic, and reorganised a jumble of files into clearly named folders (one for the car's physical model, one for the controller, one for the simulation itself, one for the visual interface, one for the tuning tools). They also rebuilt a "staging" copy of the whole project - essentially a clean, ready-to-submit version kept in sync with the official project, so that useful discoveries made here could eventually be offered back to the main codebase. This step didn't change how the car drives; it just made sure everyone was building on the same up-to-date foundation, and made every later step in this list easier to do correctly.

While doing this, they also added the ability to load a real recorded test track into the practice simulator (instead of only artificial test tracks), and added a way to see the path the car's planning software was choosing, drawn on screen. This turned out to be essential groundwork for almost everything that follows.

## 2. Fixing basic setup problems

A handful of small but blocking issues were fixed:

- A startup script referred to a file that didn't exist, and was missing a required software component, so the driving software would simply crash on startup in some situations. Fixed.
- The instructions for which "driving mode" (a simpler backup driver vs. the main advanced one) to launch weren't written down anywhere obvious. Added clear documentation.
- Recordings of test drives (cone/track maps) were being saved to a location the tools didn't actually look in, so recorded tracks couldn't be found and reloaded. Fixed the save location so recordings are always where they're expected.
- Added a real recording of an actual competition test track into the project, which became the main track used for testing for the rest of this entire series of changes.

## 3. Fixing five bugs in replaying a recorded track

With a real track recording available, the team tried to turn it into something the practice simulator could drive around - and hit five separate problems, one after another, each blocking the next from being tested:

1. **The recreated track cut across itself at track crossings.** The method used to stitch the recorded cone positions back into a smooth track got confused wherever the track crossed near itself (like a figure-eight shape), mixing up cones from two different, unrelated parts of the track. Fixed by having the software "drive" a virtual lap around the recording, one small step at a time, using the same "which side is which" logic the live car uses.
2. **The practice car would wobble or fail as soon as any reaction-time delay was simulated.** Real controllers take a small amount of time to react; when that was included in the test, the practice car would swerve wildly or fail to finish. Fixed by making the software predict a little ahead in time to account for its own delay, similar to how a real driver anticipates rather than reacting purely to what they see this instant. Also corrected a wrong number for how sharply the car's wheels can physically turn, which had been letting the practice simulator plan turns the real car can't actually make.
3. **A loaded recorded lap would immediately think it was "done" after one step.** The finish-line check was designed for tracks that go from a clear start to a clear end, but a lap is a loop - so the start and the end are right next to each other, and the software mistook "starting the lap" for "finishing the lap." Fixed by making the finish check smarter about the difference between "near the finish" and "having actually driven the whole lap."
4. **An extra, incorrect loop sometimes got added at track crossings.** Related to problem 1: at a point where the track crosses itself, the stitching process would sometimes take a wrong turn and effectively drive an extra, unnecessary loop, corrupting the shape. Fixed by having it recognise "I've already been here" and stop correctly the first time it truly completes the lap.
5. **The recreated track sometimes ran backwards.** The software guessed which direction the lap was originally driven by picking whichever guess "looked longer," which isn't actually a reliable way to tell direction. Fixed by using the real rule from racing: track-boundary markers of one colour belong on the left, the other colour on the right, relative to the direction of travel - a much more reliable signal.
6. **The recreated track would stop early on tight corners.** The "have we completed the lap" check was too easily fooled by tight hairpin turns, where the car naturally passes close to where it just was, seconds after a tight corner rather than after a full lap. It mistook a sharp corner for finishing the whole lap. Fixed by measuring "how far has the car actually travelled" instead of just "how many points have passed," so a tight corner can no longer trigger a false finish.

After all five fixes, a real recorded competition track could finally be loaded and driven around reliably in the practice simulator - the foundation needed for realistic testing for the rest of this work.

## 4. Improving the automatic tuning tools

A few behind-the-scenes improvements were made to the system that automatically searches for good driving settings:

- Added a smarter, two-stage search: a faster, rougher search first to find a promising starting area, followed by the existing careful search to fine-tune from there - like scouting a wide area quickly before zooming in.
- Fixed a scoring number that was measured in a way that didn't scale fairly between short and long test runs.
- Made sure the tuning system's history log records exactly what settings and scoring rules were active for each attempt, so that comparing an old result to a new one later doesn't accidentally compare apples to oranges.
- Wrote up a documentation page summarising the project for less technical readers.

## 5. Finding and fixing a cause of steering wobble

Watching the real car drive, the team noticed the steering oscillating (wobbling side to side), especially in corners, sometimes badly enough to eventually push the car off the racing line. The cause: the driving software was heavily rewarded for correcting its heading error quickly, but barely penalised at all for how aggressively it spun the wheels to do so - like a driver who yanks the wheel hard to correct a small drift instead of steering back smoothly. The fix rebalanced these two priorities so the car corrects itself more gently. The team also improved how "wobbling" was measured: previously it just counted how many times the steering direction reversed, which couldn't tell the difference between a tiny wiggle and a violent swing. Now it weighs each reversal by how big it was, so genuine wobbling actually shows up as a bad score instead of being invisible to the tuning process.

## 6. Retuning and expanding the staging copy

With the wobble-scoring fixed, the settings were re-tuned against the corrected scoring system. Separately, the "staging copy" of the project (mentioned in step 1) was expanded from covering just the driving/control software to mirroring the entire real software stack - perception, planning, and control together - so that in future, anyone could download this one project and run the whole self-driving system end to end, without needing a separate copy of the official codebase.

## 7. Solving the real steering "chatter" seen on the actual car

This is one of the most important fixes in this whole stretch. The real car's steering was chattering rapidly back and forth, and it took finding three separate, independent problems to fully explain it:

**Problem A: the practice simulator let the car steer faster than physically possible.** The real car's steering motor can only turn so fast per second. The practice simulator had no such limit at all, meaning all of the tuning done in it had been done against a car that could steer unrealistically fast. This alone meant tuned settings could never transfer faithfully to the real car. Fixed by adding a proper speed limit to how fast the practice car's virtual steering wheel can turn, set to match a conservative estimate of the real car's actual limit.

**Problem B: a units mistake was hiding the problem.** A record-keeping bug meant the logged steering angle was showing numbers roughly 2.3 times too large - so what actually was "the wheels are already at maximum lock" was being logged as if there was plenty of steering room left. This had been quietly hiding the real severity of the steering-saturation problem for an entire round of testing. Fixed the conversion.

**Problem C: the position sensor was updating too slowly.** This turned out to be the dominant cause. The software that tells the driving computer "here's where the car currently is" was only actually updating half as often as the steering computer checks it - so every second check, the computer was working from stale, unchanged position information. When the position value did jump to catch up, the computer suddenly saw the car as having moved much further than expected, and overcorrected hard - exactly the pattern of chatter observed on the real car. Fixed by making the position update at the full required rate, while leaving the separate cone-detection update at its slower, cheaper rate (since that doesn't need to be as fast).

Two more useful additions came out of this investigation: an optional way to simulate imperfect position-sensing (kept switched off by default, since it wasn't actually the cause here, but useful for future testing on the real car, which does have some position uncertainty), and a fix to a scoring bug where the software's "ground truth" tracking error was accidentally just re-measuring its own estimate rather than the real, true position - meaning a big chunk of the scoring system had no real anchor to reality at all when the car's own perception was slightly off. Fixed so the true error is always measured against the real, true path.

## 8. Fixing the car speeding into corners it couldn't handle

After fixing the steering-chatter causes above, a new failure pattern showed up: the car would wobble a bit early in a lap, recover, and then completely fail on the second lap - drifting three metres off the track with the steering pinned at full lock. The cause: the software that decides how fast to go only looks at the shape of the upcoming track, completely ignoring how badly the car is currently struggling. At one point in the failing log, the software actually told the car to speed up while it was already badly off-course and unable to steer any harder. Once the steering is maxed out, slowing down is the only remaining way to recover - and nothing was doing that. Four related fixes were made, together:

1. Made the "how fast should I go around this corner" calculation less sensitive to one noisy, over-sharp corner estimate throwing off the whole speed decision.
2. Added a genuine safety mechanism: if the car is currently far off the intended line or pointing the wrong way, the target speed is automatically reduced, so there's always some steering ability left in reserve to recover.
3. Added a limiter so the target speed can't increase too quickly (though it can still decrease instantly, since braking in an emergency should never be delayed).
4. Fixed an inconsistency where two different parts of the software were measuring the upcoming track slightly differently, so they now agree.

Every one of these was explicitly labelled as a **workaround**, not a real fix. The actual root problem is that the path-planning software sometimes produces a jagged, physically-impossible track shape (more on this in step 17-18 later), and these four changes exist to stop that jaggedness from causing a crash, not to remove the jaggedness itself. The team specifically noted: if that underlying planning problem is ever properly fixed, these workarounds might need to be loosened or removed - but only after re-testing, not automatically.

## 9. Fixing the scoring system to actually judge everything it should

The team ran a controlled experiment: deliberately created a handful of driving-setting profiles representing known bad behaviours (wobbling, sluggishness, etc.) and checked whether the scoring system correctly rated them as bad. It didn't. Investigating why revealed that the scoring system judges 12 different aspects of driving quality (like tracking accuracy, smoothness, speed matching, and so on), but because these 12 things naturally come in very different-sized numbers, two of them were completely dominating the score and the other ten barely mattered at all - to the point that a setting profile that deliberately wobbled scored *better* than a sensible one, purely because it happened to track the line slightly more tightly. This also likely explains why past tuning attempts had produced such wildly inconsistent results: the tuning search could wander freely on ten of the twelve measures because none of them affected the score anyway.

Fixed by rescaling each of the 12 measures to a comparable size before combining them, so each one actually has the influence it's supposed to have. A related fix changed how the scoring system handles multiple test tracks at once, softening the effect of one unlucky failure so it doesn't completely swamp everything else being measured. This was an important step, but the team noted it didn't fully solve the underlying issue (explained next).

## 10. Rebuilding the scoring system with a real "perfect lap" target

Two deeper problems with the scoring system were fixed together:

**A missing yardstick.** Part of the score rewards being fast, calculated as actual lap time compared to a "target" lap time - but that target had been a rough, generic guess that turned out to be wildly wrong (sometimes several times too generous) and different for every track, so the same quality of driving could score very differently depending purely on which track was used. Fixed by actually calculating the fastest physically possible lap time for each specific track, using real physics limits, giving a fair and consistent yardstick to measure against.

**A scoring structure that could be "gamed."** Simply adding up all the scoring measures with different weights turns out to have a fundamental flaw: no matter how the weights are adjusted, a setting profile that wobbles but tracks the line very tightly can always find a way to outscore a sensible one, because tight tracking earns credit on the same scale as everything else. The fix restructured scoring into three clear priority tiers instead of one flat sum: first and most important, did the car crash or fail to finish at all (this is now judged completely separately, so a car cannot use good tracking to "make up for" a crash); second, how close to the fastest-possible lap time it was; third, all the finer-grained quality measures from before, now used only to fine-tune between two runs that are otherwise similar. This fixed the gaming problem outright: in the same test that previously showed a bad setting outscoring a good one, the ranking is now correct.

Also fixed along the way: a separate braking-distance bug where the speed-planning software was only looking at how sharp an upcoming corner is, but not how far away it is - meaning it could ask the car to brake at physically absurd rates because it forgot to account for the distance available to slow down in. Fixed so the car's speed target now properly accounts for the distance remaining before a corner.

A follow-up review shortly after this change caught three real loose ends: the visual interface tool was still silently using the old, less accurate lap-time target instead of the new one (now fixed so both match); several documentation pages still described the old scoring scale and would have been actively misleading to anyone reading them (updated); and the full-project "staging copy" mentioned earlier had drifted slightly out of sync with the real changes (re-synced and double-checked).

## 11. Comparing the practice simulator directly against the real car

With scoring now trustworthy, the team turned to directly investigating why the real car still drove noticeably worse than the practice simulator predicted, even using the exact same tuned settings. They added detailed timing logs to the real car's software to see exactly how fresh its position information was at every instant, moment to moment. Comparing a normal test run to a run where the car struggled badly, they found something striking: in the bad run, the position information was refreshing only a third as often as normal, and at one point froze completely for almost a full second while the car was moving at highway speed - meaning the car drove blind for a significant distance and then had to violently correct itself once fresh information arrived. The practice simulator, by contrast, always assumed perfectly fresh position information every single instant, so this kind of failure could never even occur inside it.

The team built a model of this "position freezing" behaviour into the practice simulator, carefully tuned to match the measured real-world frequency and duration of these freezes. Having done that, they tested it - and reported honestly that, on its own, this did not meaningfully close the gap between practice and real-world driving. In the same investigation, several other possible causes were tested and ruled out too: the practice car's grip/tyre behaviour, how fast it entered corners, the quality of its path-planning, and a few others - none of them, individually, explained the difference either.

## 12. Ruling out tyre-related explanations

The next candidate investigated was whether the practice simulator's tyres simply had different amounts of "grip" than the real car. The team adjusted the practice simulator's tyre-grip setting until it exactly matched one specific real-world measurement (how much the real car's steering angle needed to increase for a given cornering force) - and it matched perfectly. But when that same adjusted setting was checked against two other, independent real-world measurements, it failed both of them badly. Two different measurements of what should be the same underlying physical property disagreed with each other, which is a sign that grip itself isn't the real explanation - some other, more specific mechanism is being mistaken for a grip problem. Pushing the grip setting around further only ever produced a different kind of failure (the practice car sliding off track), not a better match - while the real car was observed comfortably holding full steering lock for over a second at forces well within any reasonable grip limit, which a genuine grip problem would not allow. Grip was ruled out.

The next candidate: maybe the steering command sent to the real car simply isn't producing as much actual turning as expected, uniformly, regardless of speed - like a mechanical mismatch in how "25 degrees of steering" is defined between the two systems. Careful measurement from real driving logs, working backwards from how much the car actually rotated versus how much steering was commanded, showed this wasn't a flat, constant mismatch either - the shortfall got dramatically worse at higher speeds, which a simple constant mismatch cannot explain. This ruled out that hypothesis too, along with a few smaller candidates: steering delay, a sensor/logging error, and an imbalance between front and rear tyre grip (achieving the measured effect through that route would require the front tyres to be roughly ten times less grippy than physically realistic, which isn't credible). By this point the team explicitly noted: the car's overall cornering force looks about right, but its actual rotation doesn't - a pattern no ordinary tyre model can produce. Something else is going on, and the recommended next step was a much more controlled, isolated experiment rather than another guess.

## 13. Finding the real cause: FSDS secretly limits hard cornering

The team built a careful, isolated experiment: hold the steering wheel at a fixed angle, at a fixed speed, with the self-driving software's decision-making disconnected entirely, and just watch what the car actually does. Repeated across many speeds and steering angles.

The result was decisive. Below roughly 5 metres per second, the real simulator (FSDS) does exactly what's asked - full steering produces full turning, precisely as expected. But above that speed, something clamps down hard: nearly doubling the steering command barely changes how sharply the car actually turns. This isn't a gradual, gentle effect - it's closer to a hard ceiling that switches on above a certain speed. Comparing several possible explanations mathematically, a "cornering-force ceiling" fit the real data far better than tyre understeer, a speed-scaled steering system, or any constant mismatch. And critically, this isn't a tyre-grip limit either: the cornering force where the car hits this ceiling is well below what the same car is measured reaching elsewhere on a normal lap, and a genuine grip limit wouldn't depend on speed the way this clearly does.

This directly explains the steering-chatter problem from the very start of this stretch of work: the driving software plans a corner, the real simulator secretly clamps how sharply the car can actually turn, the car doesn't turn as much as planned, heading error builds up, the software responds by demanding even more steering, and the wheels end up pinned at their mechanical limit on every single lap.

The next open question was exactly how this limit is enforced internally - a hard ceiling, a steering system that gets weaker at speed, or an active "stability control"-style correction - since telling these apart needed a different kind of test (a sudden steering step, rather than a steady hold), which is the subject of the next step.

## 14. Modelling the discovered limit inside the practice simulator

A follow-up experiment - suddenly stepping the steering from neutral to a fixed angle and watching the car's response over time - showed the car briefly overshooting past the eventual limit before settling back down to it. A hard, instant ceiling couldn't produce an overshoot like that; only something that takes a small amount of time to "kick in" could. This pinned down that the real cornering-force limit is enforced gradually, over roughly a few tenths of a second, and also confirmed precisely what it holds constant: cornering force itself (not, as first suspected, the car's turning rate), since the measured cornering force stayed nearly the same across different test speeds while the turning rate did not.

This was then built into the practice simulator's model of the car as a gradual, physically-motivated turning-force limiter, rather than an instant hard clip - because an instant clip would get the end result right but would remove the realistic "ramping up" behaviour that the driving software actually needs to react to while planning a corner.

Getting the exact settings right took two attempts. The first attempt set how quickly the limit "kicks in" based on how much the real car's overshoot suggested - but this made the limit act too slowly, meaning it barely engaged before a real corner was already over, allowing the practice car to run off track. The team caught this immediately and refit the timing to match the *peak* force measured instead, which reacts fast enough to actually matter during a turn. The second attempt found that fitting the limit's strength to the real car's *typical, steady* cornering force made it too strict compared to reality - it turns out the real limit is best understood as a "sustained average" cap that the real car does briefly exceed sometimes, not an absolute wall. Refitting the strength to match the real car's occasional *peak* moments (rather than its average) fixed this, and the practice car could finally complete a full lap with the new limit switched on, with every measured driving statistic moving closer to matching the real car - though not all the way. The team was careful to report this as "an improvement, not a full fix": the real car still hits its steering limit roughly three times more often than the improved practice simulator did.

## 15. Fixing a subtle mathematical flaw in how the limit was modelled

Looking more closely at why the model couldn't be made both accurate at its resting point and accurate at its peak simultaneously, the team found a structural mathematical issue: the way the limit had been built could only ever settle at a value slightly *above* the true target, no matter how it was tuned - the harder you tried to fix one aspect (the steady value), the worse the other aspect (the peak) got, and vice versa. This wasn't two separate mistakes; it was one underlying design flaw showing up two different ways.

The fix replaced the underlying approach with a mathematically different one that is, by its very construction, guaranteed to settle exactly at the correct value regardless of how strongly it's tuned - leaving only one remaining setting (how quickly it reacts) to be tuned freely, without that tuning fighting against getting the resting value right. Tested against a long, sustained cornering scenario the fix hadn't been tuned against at all, the improved version was roughly five times more accurate. This visibly improved how realistically hard cornering forces are distributed in the practice simulator, but - again reported honestly - only moved the actual steering-saturation frequency (how often the wheels are pinned at their limit) by a small amount, far short of fully closing the gap to the real car. Two more possible explanations for the remaining gap were also tested and ruled out in the same step: making the cornering limit even stricter just causes the practice car to slide off track rather than reproducing the real symptom, and a separate known difference in how the two systems handle acceleration commands turned out not to be a contributing cause either, once checked carefully.

Right after this, the exact timing of "how quickly the limit kicks in" was re-measured with a more careful, longer version of the earlier stepped-steering test, and refit to a tighter, more confident number. This refinement, on its own, did not further close the remaining gap either - if anything it moved a fraction of a percent in the opposite direction - but was kept anyway because it makes the practice simulator's *physical accuracy* better, which is a separate goal from closing the specific saturation gap, and the team was explicit that this parameter had already been ruled out as the source of the remaining difference.

## 16. Properly measuring how much of the gap is actually explained

Up to this point, every single test of "does this factor help" had been measured as one before-and-after number on the one real recorded track - with no sense of how much that number would naturally bounce around just from re-running things. Since the practice simulator is built to be perfectly repeatable on that one track (deliberately, so that the automatic tuning search gets a stable, fair score to work with), simply re-running the same test twice would always report exactly zero variation - which is misleadingly reassuring, not genuinely informative.

To fix this, the team built a proper testing tool that runs every previously-tested factor across five different artificial tracks with different shapes, using the differences between those five as a genuine measure of natural variation, alongside the one real-world-comparable number from the recorded track. While building this, they also caught and corrected a mistake before it could cause confusion: an old, outdated "baseline" number from much earlier in the project was at risk of being compared directly against a new number measured completely differently - which would have been comparing two things that aren't actually the same, exactly the kind of subtle mistake this whole careful-measurement effort exists to prevent. Fixed by re-measuring today's true baseline with today's tools, and using that consistently from this point onward.

The result was sobering: every single factor tested and modelled so far - the cornering-force limit's exact mathematical form, its strength, and its timing - has an effect on the real gap that is smaller than the natural run-to-run variation seen across the five test tracks. None of them, individually, are confidently distinguishable from random noise. Even adding together the two largest, most independent effects found so far only closes at most about a quarter of the total known gap. In other words: at least three-quarters of why the real car drives worse than the practice simulator predicts is still unexplained by anything discovered up to this point. One genuinely useful, more detailed finding did come out of this step, though: breaking the results down by individual track shape (rather than just averaging across all five) showed that the cornering-limit fix has essentially zero effect at all on two of the five track shapes, and its entire measured benefit is concentrated on tracks with sustained, moderate, high-speed bends - consistent with the idea that this particular limit only ever matters above a certain speed, and giving future investigation a much sharper, more specific place to look.

## 17. Checking whether the path-planning itself was causing sudden direction changes

With the cornering-limit avenue thoroughly measured and mostly exhausted, attention turned to an earlier, still-open idea: since the path-planning software recalculates the car's intended route completely from scratch on every single instant (rather than gradually updating an existing plan), maybe that process itself was occasionally producing sudden, unrealistic jumps in the direction the car is told to point - jumps neither car could ever physically turn fast enough to follow, which would build up heading error and explain much of the remaining gap.

Two specific possible causes of this were tested directly:

**A "reset" behaviour in the path-smoothing logic**, which is designed to snap to a fresh plan if the car's calculated path moves too far between instants (rather than blending smoothly), and could in principle cause a big, sudden heading jump if triggered. Confirmed this mechanism genuinely exists and works identically in both the practice and real systems, but careful measurement on the specific real test track being used found it essentially never actually triggers there - the track just never produces movements big enough to set it off. Since it never fires on this track, it cannot be responsible for the gap measured on this track, though the team noted it could still matter on a different, more demanding track shape in future.

**Ordinary smoothing "noise"** in the path-planning, separate from the reset case above - checking whether the everyday, non-triggering version of the smoothing process was still injecting enough of its own wobble into the intended direction to matter. Measuring this directly against how fast the intended direction was actually changing found only a very weak connection - far too weak to explain the large majority of heading-error growth that earlier analysis had attributed to path-planning issues in general.

With both of these specific mechanisms checked and cleared, the investigation was pointed back toward a different, already-known issue: the underlying shape of the path itself - not how it's smoothed moment-to-moment - which is picked up directly in the next step.

## 18. Fixing a cone-mapping bug and adding a way to simulate imperfect vision

While investigating the path-shape issue above, the team found and fixed a genuine, previously-unknown bug: when the car spotted a completely new track-boundary cone for the first time, and happened to detect it twice within the same instant (which can genuinely happen), both detections were being permanently kept as two separate cones at nearly the same spot, instead of being recognised as the same cone and merged into one. This could quietly corrupt the car's memory of the track's shape over time. Fixed everywhere this code exists (the real car's software, the practice simulator, and the staging copy), and carefully verified the fix doesn't change anything when the bug wasn't actually happening, while correctly merging duplicates whenever it was.

Separately, the team added an optional way to simulate a small amount of realistic error in cone detection (switched off by default). This matters because, right now, the practice simulator assumes the car's "vision" of the track boundaries is perfect - it always knows exactly where every cone is, with no noise or mistakes at all - which the real car's actual camera/sensor system cannot achieve. This new option makes it possible, in future, to test whether imperfect real-world vision is part of the explanation for the remaining gap; on its own, at a realistic level of imperfection, it did not noticeably change the results in this round of testing, but the tool now exists to test that properly with real measured data later.

A completely unrelated, but important, bug was also found and fixed in the same step: a long-standing configuration file had a rule that was meant to exclude one specific temporary folder, but was accidentally written in a way that also silently hid an entire mirrored copy of important project files from the team's usual "what's changed" checks - without anyone noticing, for an unknown length of time. This didn't affect how the car drives, but it means that checking "does this file already exist somewhere" needs to actually look at the files directly from now on, not just rely on the usual change-tracking tool, since that tool had been unreliable in this specific spot.

## 19. Fixing startup scripts, finally unblocking a real test drive

Two stacked technical bugs in the startup script used to launch a live test (connecting the driving software to the real simulator) had been silently blocking every attempt to run a real test this session. First, the script itself had a formatting mistake near the top that meant it wasn't actually running the way it was supposed to, which caused a later check to always fail. Second, and more subtly: the team's test computer runs inside a compatibility layer (WSL) on Windows, and that layer cannot reach the simulator running on the Windows side using the usual, simple network address - it needs a different, specific address instead, which the connecting software already knew how to use, but which had simply never been set. Fixing both, and applying the identical fix to the second, mirrored copy of the same startup script, finally allowed the very first real, live test run of this entire investigation to happen.

## 20. The first real side-by-side comparison, and researching the simulator's internals

With a live test finally possible, the team ran one and directly compared it to the original baseline measurement from the very start of the investigation. The result looked promising - the steering-saturation problem was noticeably reduced - but there was an important catch: this test run happened to have fifteen different, separate changes all active in the software at once, so there was no way yet to tell which specific change (or combination) actually deserved the credit for the improvement. This ambiguity became the very next thing to resolve.

Separately, the team researched how the underlying simulator's game engine (Unreal Engine, with a physics system called PhysX) typically handles vehicle steering, looking for a known, off-the-shelf setting that could explain the discovered cornering limit. They found a real, documented feature matching the exact behaviour observed (normal steering at low speed, reduced at high speed) that vehicle templates in this engine commonly include - a very plausible explanation. However, the actual configuration file that would confirm this couldn't be opened directly, because of a technical limitation in how large files are currently stored and synced in this project; confirming this fully will require opening the simulator's project directly in its full editor software, which wasn't available in this session.

## 21. Correcting an overstated number, then properly isolating the real cause

Two follow-up steps closed out this stretch of work.

First, revisiting an earlier claim that a large percentage of the drive showed the path-planning "jumping" problem from step 17, the team discovered the measurement itself was flawed - it was mostly picking up an entirely ordinary, harmless side effect of how the path is represented internally, not a real problem. A corrected, more careful measurement found the real effect is much smaller than first reported, though still real in a couple of specific, genuine instances. This was an important, humbling check: a measurement that doesn't change even after attempting a fix doesn't necessarily mean the fix failed - sometimes it means the measurement itself was never measuring the right thing.

Second, the team designed and ran a proper controlled experiment to finally isolate which of the many recent changes actually explained the improvement seen in step 20. They deliberately, temporarily reintroduced one specific old bug (the position-update-rate problem fixed back in step 7) on the real car, ran a live test, and then immediately reverted it back to the fixed version. The result confirmed that this specific fix genuinely matters a great deal in isolation - reintroducing the old bug made things clearly worse - but also revealed something less tidy: the result was worse than *both* the improved run and the very original baseline, meaning the different changes made throughout this whole stretch of work don't simply add up in a predictable, simple way. The test also directly reproduced, for the first time end-to-end on video/log rather than just inferred from separate pieces of evidence, the exact real-world symptom that kicked off this entire investigation: the car drifting wide off the track and slowly correcting itself, with the steering pinned at full lock the whole time.

Finally, before fully trusting any of these numbers, the team ran one more sanity check: repeating one of the "improved" test conditions five times in a row instead of just once. The repeated result came back clearly worse than the original single "improved" run, and even worse than the very first baseline measurement from the start of the investigation - revealing that results on the real car vary quite a lot from run to run, purely by chance, and that a single test is not reliable evidence on its own. In the process of checking this, they also caught and fixed a small mistake in their own quick-analysis tool, which had been mistaking a glitch in how "one lap" gets detected for a real (but implausibly short) lap. With that fixed, the honest conclusion stands: real-world steering saturation on this car, at this fixed setup, genuinely varies between roughly 15% and 32% from run to run, and no single test run in this entire investigation is reliable enough on its own to be called "the" result. This doesn't erase what was learned - the earlier finding that a specific settings change wasn't responsible for the improvement still holds regardless, and the position-update-rate problem is still clearly visible and real - but it does mean the *exact size* of some effects needs to be re-measured with repeated tests before being trusted. The stretch of work closes with the team explicitly writing this down as a lesson for next time: a single test result, even one that tells a very convincing story, is not proof by itself.

## Where things stand now, in plain terms

By the end of this stretch of work:

- **One real, confirmed cause has been found and modelled**: the real simulator secretly limits how much cornering force the car can sustain above a certain speed, and this has now been built into the practice simulator reasonably accurately.
- **Several other plausible causes have been carefully ruled out**, including tyre grip, general understeer, a scaling mistake in the steering command, sensor delay, and a few smaller candidates - each ruled out by direct, careful measurement rather than assumption.
- **Several real, unrelated bugs were found and fixed along the way**, including a steering-speed limit that didn't exist, a units mistake that hid a problem for a whole testing cycle, a position-update-rate mismatch, a duplicate-cone bug, a hidden-files configuration bug, and several bugs in replaying a recorded track - each fixed properly and in every place it needed to be fixed.
- **Even after all of that, most of the actual gap between the practice simulator and the real car remains unexplained.** The best-measured fix so far accounts for at most about a quarter of the difference. The leading remaining suspect is a defect in exactly how the path-planning software shapes the car's intended route, which is real, confirmed, and not yet fixed.
- **The team has learned, and explicitly written down, some general lessons for how to keep investigating carefully**: don't trust a single test result, make sure a measurement is actually measuring the thing you think it is before concluding a fix failed, and always compare like with like when judging whether something got better or worse.
